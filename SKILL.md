# Source Geometry Triptych

Create one coherent vertical artwork showing a disciplined three-stage transformation of a user-supplied image:

1. Top: preserve the original source image as photographic material.
2. Middle: abstract, simplify, and reconstruct the source with black geometric linework on a light neutral field.
3. Bottom: develop the middle section's formal system into an art-directed color composition and remove all black outlines.

Use the image-generation/editing capability. If a local source image has not yet been visually inspected, view it before generating. Include every target image through the supported reference-image mechanism and do not recreate the source from memory.

## Format, View Normalization, and Registration

Recommend a borderless 2:3 vertical final canvas, such as 1024 x 1536. Divide it into exactly three equal horizontal panels. Each panel must have a 2:1 width-to-height ratio; stacking three 2:1 panels produces the 2:3 final canvas.

Treat a 2:1 source crop as the preferred input composition. When the supplied image is not 2:1, adapt it without stretching:

- Prefer a visually safe 2:1 crop when it preserves the subject, focal relationships, and essential context.
- If cropping would remove essential content, preserve the original image at its native aspect ratio within the panel rather than synthesizing, extending, or recomposing photographic content.
- Do not distort proportions or silently amputate important parts merely to reach the ratio.

The top panel keeps the source photograph unchanged in content and viewpoint. It may only be proportionally scaled and safely cropped to fit the panel; do not rotate, warp, perspective-correct, outpaint, extend, retouch, redraw, relight, or reconstruct it. Convert the subject only in the middle and bottom panels into the same source-appropriate orthographic view and shared formal system. Preserve the source's dominant structural orientation while removing camera foreshortening, lens distortion, oblique perspective, and accidental photographic overlap in those transformed panels only.

Choose the canonical orthographic view that best matches the input and most clearly exposes the subject's structure:

- predominantly frontal input: use a front elevation;
- predominantly left- or right-facing input: use the corresponding left or right orthographic elevation rather than rotating it to the front;
- a dominant back, top, underside, facade, or other structural plane: use the matching rear, top, bottom, or planar orthographic view;
- flowers, butterflies, leaves, ferns, fans, and other planar or radial subjects: present the photographed primary structural plane square to the viewer;
- multiple subjects or a subject using a prop: choose the view that best preserves the photographed action, roles, relative scale, and essential relationship while clarifying avoidable depth overlap;
- an input genuinely between standard views: choose the nearest informative orthographic view and retain only the smallest local reveal needed for an identity-bearing feature.

Do not change a recognizable side, rear, or top view into a front view merely because frontality is conventional. View normalization should clarify the photographed orientation, not replace it.

The transformed panels may normalize pose, remove perspective, and purposefully simplify, combine, scale, rotate, overlap, crop, or relocate extracted geometric modules to improve the composition. They must remain unmistakably derived from the source through subject identity, characteristic parts, essential action or relationship, dominant direction, and a few source-specific proportions or accents. Exact contour, placement, spacing, and one-to-one photographic correspondence are not required. Do not invent a generic specimen unrelated to the photographed subject. Panels run edge to edge across the full canvas width. Do not add an outer border, side padding, top/bottom margin, title area, or mockup frame. Use at most a hairline neutral separator between panels.

Plan the shared crop around a **subject integrity envelope**: mark the subject's outer silhouette, supporting/contact points, characteristic appendages, focal features, and any identity-bearing accessory or prop. Keep this envelope safely inside the middle and bottom panels. Do not let abstraction, panel boundaries, or oversized geometry cut off, omit, fuse, or ambiguously dissolve an essential part. Background elements may be cropped or reduced more aggressively. Intentional occlusion is acceptable only when it already exists in the source and the overlap order remains legible.

Give the integrity envelope a visible safety margin rather than placing extremities exactly on a panel edge. Check the four canvas directions independently. For standing or walking figures, treat feet, paws, wheels, bases, or other ground contacts as protected anchors: retain their full outer shape, their attachment to the limb or body, the negative space around them, and a small amount of ground beneath them. Never let a panel boundary, foreground pattern, or color merger shave off toes, heels, soles, or contact shadows needed to explain stance.

## Interpret the Source

Identify the image's structural idea before choosing shapes: dominant masses, directional forces, silhouette, overlaps, rhythm, focal tension, and negative space. Preserve recognition through relationships rather than detail. Reduce incidental texture and small objects unless one is essential to the image's identity.

Establish a clear **subject-background hierarchy** before abstracting. Explicitly identify the primary subject element or subject group, including any prop that is functionally part of its action. Organize the transformed composition around that subject as the visual center of attention, without requiring mechanical geometric centering. Give the subject the strongest silhouette, most complete local-whole construction, clearest overlap logic, highest concentration of informative secondary geometry, and strongest useful contrast. Treat the background as supporting structure rather than a second subject:

- merge repeated foliage, stones, architecture, flowers, ground texture, or clutter into fewer and larger masses;
- retain only dominant background axes, framing shapes, contact planes, and one or two rhythms that reinforce the subject;
- omit small background objects, repeated micro-contours, surface texture, and incidental gaps unless they materially affect recognition or composition;
- preserve necessary contextual relationships, such as the surface under a figure, a prop being used, or a branch supporting an animal, but simplify everything beyond that role more aggressively;
- use unequal information density: the subject may carry useful secondary detail, while the background should usually remain at primary geometry with sparse secondary accents.

In the transformed panels, use a moderate, authored simplification rather than either detailed illustration or an icon. Reduce the subject to a compact set of primary forms while retaining enough informative secondary geometry to explain structure, articulation, material logic, characteristic pattern, and two or three source-specific relationships. Preserve selective repeated modules when their rhythm identifies the subject, such as a small bracket sequence, a few window bays, essential facial divisions, or a restrained wing-band pattern. Omit surface texture, purely ornamental subdivision, repeated micro-structure, and descriptive interior detail that does not improve identity or construction. Continue to compress the background into one to three broad framing masses, support planes, or directional rhythms whenever the scene permits; use lighter or thinner line weight, broader color fields, lower contrast, reduced saturation, and softer transitions. The subject must remain crisp, dominant, and structurally specific without retaining near-photographic detail.

Do not erase the setting into an empty void when it supplies balance, direction, scale, or support. The goal is a quieter background with legible structural cues, not subject extraction.

Construct the abstraction primarily from recognizable standard geometric forms: triangles, circles, ellipses, squares, rectangles, regular or controlled polygons, straight lines, arcs, and points. Use these forms as intact primitives, repeated modules, overlapping or nested combinations, cropped/intersected constructions, or controlled deformations. A viewer should be able to perceive the underlying geometric family rather than only a smoothed silhouette. Prefer a small coherent vocabulary over many unrelated shapes.

Translate source features into geometric operations instead of tracing contours: a head may become an ellipse joined to a muzzle circle; a torso may use an oval, rectangle, or softened polygon; limbs, stems, branches, or antennae may use tapered rectangles, lines, or linked modules; petals, wings, leaves, and ears may use repeated ellipses, triangles, sectors, or mirrored polygons; joints, eyes, centers, and focal accents may use circles or points. Preserve recognition through proportion, repetition, alignment, symmetry/asymmetry, overlap, tangency, subtraction, and negative space.

Use planes and polygons for architectural or angular subjects; circles, ellipses, arcs, and softened modules for organic subjects; restrained hybrids when the tension between systems is meaningful. Controlled deformation may bend, taper, stretch, round, truncate, or intersect a standard form, but it must not dissolve into freehand contour drawing. Avoid mechanically tracing every edge.

For organic subjects, default to a softened geometric language. Favor circles, ellipses, rounded rectangles, broad arcs, chamfered or rounded polygons, tangent joins, and curved transitions. Use acute triangles, needle-like wedges, sawtooth contours, and clusters of sharp corners only when the source genuinely depends on tension or pointed anatomy. Resolve necessary points with a broader apex or an arc transition so the result remains geometric without becoming brittle or aggressive. Balance curves with a smaller number of straight structural axes; do not turn softness into amorphous blobs.

Do not apply smoothing uniformly. Preserve a small set of **characteristic contour events**—such as an ear break, brow angle, muzzle corner, paw joint, garment seam, tail bend, or contact-plane change—when they carry identity, articulation, or direction. Use a soft-hard-soft rhythm: broad curves for mass, short straights or controlled corners for structure, then curved transitions to reconnect the form. If rounding removes a landmark or makes neighboring parts indistinguishable, restore the most informative breakpoint rather than adding texture.

Organize important regions as **local wholes** before subdividing them. Typical local wholes include head, muzzle/face, torso, each foot or paw, tail, hand, wheel, garment, or identity-bearing prop. For each local whole:

1. establish one enclosing mass with a clear silhouette and attachment to the larger subject;
2. preserve its internal axis, overlap order, support/contact function, and one or two characteristic landmarks;
3. add only the minimum secondary shapes needed to articulate the local structure;
4. make its internal geometry share proportions, curve radii, alignments, or tangencies so it reads as one designed unit rather than loose pieces.

Do not let the primary-mass target cause several distinct local wholes to collapse into an ambiguous blob. Conversely, do not fragment a head, foot, or other small whole into unrelated decorative primitives.

Calibrate the degree of abstraction before generating. Preserve enough structure that the source remains recognizable through silhouette, proportion, overlap, focal features, and directional forces, but remove incidental detail, texture, and repeated micro-contours. Aim for a designed system rather than either an icon or a trace.

Use a three-level hierarchy:

- Primary geometry: usually 4-7 dominant masses or directional structures that carry identity and balance.
- Secondary geometry: a restrained supporting set that explains overlap, articulation, modular rhythm, material structure, and two or three characteristic features.
- Tertiary accents: a few deliberate marks may preserve a focal feature, characteristic pattern, or controlled irregularity; they must never become texture replacement.

Do not treat these counts as rigid quotas. Increase complexity for layered scenes and reduce it for simple subjects, but keep a clear primary-secondary-tertiary hierarchy. If the result could describe many unrelated images, it is too simple. If it resembles edge detection, technical tracing, or a coloring book, it is too complex.

Extract a compact palette from perceptually important source colors, not merely the most frequent pixels. Treat these colors as anchors rather than immutable samples. Harmonize hue, value, saturation, temperature, and proportion when this improves hierarchy, rhythm, adjacency, or emotional tone. Preserve enough source color identity to connect the reconstruction to the photograph, but allow restrained art-directed shifts, chromatic neutrals, and more coherent dominant-support-accent relationships. Avoid arbitrary recoloring and fashionable palettes unrelated to the source.

Always read [references/aesthetic-criteria.md](references/aesthetic-criteria.md) before composing the generation prompt. It contains complexity checks and art-historical direction. Also read and follow [references/production-workflow.md](references/production-workflow.md); its shape budget, staged-generation sequence, and stop conditions are mandatory, including for batch jobs.

## Plan the Reconstruction Before Prompting

Do not ask the image generator to "simplify," "geometrize," or "make line art" without first defining a concrete reconstruction. Before writing the generation prompt, make a short internal construction plan with these five items:

1. **Subject and canonical view:** identify the primary subject element or group, state the source-appropriate orthographic view—front, left, right, rear, top, bottom, or primary structural plane—and name the photographic perspective cues that must be removed. Explain why this view best preserves the input's dominant orientation and identity.
2. **Primitive inventory:** map every primary local whole to a perceptible standard form or controlled variant. For example: head ellipse, muzzle overlapping circle or ellipse, torso rounded rectangle or ellipse, limbs tapered rounded rectangles, paws ellipses, ears softened triangles or sectors, tail linked arcs or tapered modules, and accessories as nested bands, rectangles, circles, or controlled polygons.
3. **Inherent geometric rules:** inspect the subject for bilateral symmetry, central or rotational symmetry, radial organization, translation, repetition, alternation, grids, modular ratios, or regular spacing. Record which rules are genuinely present and preserve them in the construction. Do not impose symmetry or repetition that the subject does not have.
4. **Construction operations:** name the useful overlap, tangency, alignment, mirroring, repetition, intersection, subtraction, rotation, or scale relationship between those primitives. The result must read as assembled geometry, not as a traced silhouette with decorative divisions.
5. **Transformation map:** identify the final simplified closed shapes, axes, modules, overlaps, and negative spaces in the middle panel. Treat that geometry as a locked template for the bottom panel. The bottom may assign color, value, and limited tonal variation to those same regions, but may not merge, split, shift, scale, rotate, add, remove, or redraw their boundaries.

Add a sixth required item:

6. **Shape budget and deletion list:** choose a subject-appropriate maximum for primary masses, secondary shapes, repeated modules, background regions, and background axes using `production-workflow.md`. Name the photographic details that must disappear. Treat the budget as a ceiling, not a target. Do not generate until every retained module has a structural purpose and the deletion list is explicit.

Treat this plan as a hard prerequisite. If the prompt cannot name the canonical view, primary primitives, construction relationships, shape budget, and deletion list concretely, revise the plan before generating.

Use a **subject-and-primitive-first prompt order**: identify the subject, chosen source-appropriate orthographic view, standard forms, and inherent geometric rules before mentioning texture, background, palette, or style. Preserve the photographed orientation, action, and directional logic while rebuilding perspective-dependent relationships in the selected orthographic view.

### Reject contour translation

Line-art conversion, vector-like tracing, coloring-book contours, fur-edge tracing, polygonization of the photographic silhouette, and ordinary stylization do not satisfy this skill. A recognizable outline is insufficient. The middle panel must visibly expose a small family of standard primitives and the operations joining them. Source-specific contour breaks may modify the primitives locally, but may not replace the primitive construction.

## Compose the Three Stages

Use the fixed 2:3 canvas and three equal 2:1 panels defined above. The middle and bottom panels must use the same source-appropriate orthographic construction in exact registration. The bottom is the middle panel's simplified geometry filled with color: every enclosing contour, internal division, overlap boundary, axis-derived region, and negative-space boundary must correspond one-to-one. Do not recompose, merge, split, shift, scale, rotate, add, remove, or redraw shapes between these panels. Preserve correspondence with the unchanged top photograph through identity, orientation, characteristic parts, action, and directional structure.

The top panel must use the supplied source photograph itself without any generative alteration. Preserve its original viewpoint, perspective, lighting, subject geometry, background, and photographic appearance. Only proportional scaling and a safe edge crop are allowed for ratio adaptation; never outpaint, extend, warp, perspective-correct, restage, relight, retouch, redraw, or stylize it.

In the middle panel, draw only purposeful black lines on white, off-white, or very light neutral. Use consistent or intentionally tiered stroke weights. Build the selected orthographic view from visibly resolved standard geometric forms and controlled variants: circles, ellipses, triangles, squares, rectangles, polygons, arcs, lines, and points. Let combination, overlap, tangency, repetition, mirroring, rotation, scaling, intersection, subtraction, and restrained deformation perform the abstraction. Do not replace this constructive geometry with a traced outer contour or freehand illustration. Preserve the complete subject integrity envelope: all identity-bearing masses, limbs or supports, appendages, focal features, and characteristic accessories must be present and spatially connected in a legible way, with protected extremities and ground contacts fully inside the panel. Construct recognizable local wholes—especially head/face and feet/supports—as coherent enclosing modules before adding internal divisions. Use a compact but sufficiently descriptive shape system: merge weak or repetitive detail, yet retain a small number of source-specific structural subdivisions, repeated modules, facial or pattern regions, joints, openings, seams, or contact transitions when they materially improve recognition and character. Omit a line only when its removal does not weaken identity, articulation, material logic, rhythm, action, support, or the chosen geometric rule. Simplify the background into one to three large framing masses, support planes, directional axes, or rhythmic cues, using far fewer lines and quieter weight than the subject. Do not trace background foliage, debris, repeated flowers, stones, bark, paving, architecture, ornament, or surface texture element by element. Let major contours continue through clean arcs, tangencies, rounded corners, deliberate overlaps, and informative structural breaks. Leave generous breathing room without collapsing the subject into a generic symbol. Do not add fills of any color, solid black masses, gray shading, hatching, gradients, shadows, labels, arrows, captions, mockup framing, or ornamental marks. Eyes, noses, and other dark source features must remain hollow outlines in this panel.

In the bottom panel, use the middle panel as a locked color-by-region template. Fill every simplified middle-panel region in exact registration while preserving the same outer contours, internal divisions, overlap boundaries, negative spaces, placement, scale, and orientation. Do not add detail, redraw contours, alter the silhouette, merge or split regions, move modules, or introduce new shapes. The only transformation from middle to bottom is removal of black lines and assignment of flat source-related color fields, with limited value adjustment needed to keep adjacent regions legible. Preserve every protected local whole and contact relationship through value contrast, color adjacency, or narrow breathing gaps. Make the subject dominant through clearer value separation and restrained source-related accents. Keep the already simplified background as the same one to three broad regions with quieter color and fewer accents. Remove all black contour lines and do not simulate outlines with dark borders. Prefer flat color; subtle tonal variation is allowed only inside an existing region and may not create a new boundary.

The result should feel like an authored foundation-studio or visual-communication study: conceptually legible, compositionally alive, materially economical, and visually refined. Build artistry through a coherent shape family, varied scale, repetition with variation, curved-versus-straight rhythm, intentional tangencies and interruptions, active negative space, asymmetric balance, and a clear visual center. Avoid evenly distributing shapes, mechanically centering every form, or decorating empty areas with unrelated geometry. Each secondary form should reinforce silhouette, movement, counterweight, overlap, or focal emphasis. Draw from art-historical principles selectively: constructive geometry, dynamic asymmetry, figure-ground tension, rhythmic repetition, optical balance, cut-paper economy, and intelligent color interaction. Use references as methods, not surface costumes. Never reproduce a specific artwork or ask for an exact imitation of any artist, especially a living artist.

## Generate and Review

Use the staged workflow in `production-workflow.md`. Build and approve the middle-panel geometric master before producing the color reconstruction. Derive the bottom panel from that locked master with a template-preserving edit or deterministic region coloring whenever available. Assemble the unchanged source photograph and approved transformed panels deterministically whenever practical; do not ask a generative model to recreate the source photograph. A one-pass triptych is a fallback only when the available image tool cannot support staged references or assembly, and it remains subject to every review gate.

Write generation prompts in concrete visual terms. State the selected source-appropriate orthographic transformation, exact shape budget, explicit deletion list, standard-shape vocabulary, applicable symmetry or repetition rules, subject integrity envelope, middle-panel no-fill rule, bottom-panel no-outline rule, background limit, and palette-harmonization logic. For a one-pass fallback, also state the 2:3 canvas, three equal 2:1 edge-to-edge panels, unchanged source-photo top panel, and exact one-to-one middle-to-bottom registration. Ask for one coherent final artifact, not unrelated outputs.

Order the prompt by priority so the generator does not substitute photographic fidelity for analysis:

1. primary subject and source-appropriate orthographic view;
2. named primitive inventory, inherent geometric rules, and construction operations;
3. transformation map and chosen constructive recomposition strategy;
4. subject integrity and recognizable relationships;
5. middle no-fill and bottom no-outline material rules;
6. background simplification or softening and art-directed source-related palette.

Do not overload the prompt with low-priority background detail. When constraints compete, simplify, soften, or omit background information before weakening the chosen orthographic view, subject emphasis, inherent geometric rules, or primitive construction.

After every stage, inspect the result. A failed gate is a stop condition: reject the artifact, rebuild from the construction plan, and retry the current image before coloring, assembling, saving, delivering, or proceeding to the next image. This rule applies to batch tasks; do not accept first-pass failures merely because other images are complete. Retry when any essential invariant fails:

- the top is not the actual supplied source, its viewpoint or perspective changes, or it is cropped unsafely, warped, extended, retouched, relit, redrawn, or visibly restyled;
- the final canvas is not 2:3, a panel is not 2:1, or an outer white border remains;
- the bottom does not follow the middle panel's simplified contours and region boundaries in exact one-to-one registration, or the normalized subject is no longer recognizably derived from the top photograph;
- the chosen view contradicts the source's dominant orientation, or camera perspective and foreshortening remain dominant instead of a clear source-appropriate orthographic view;
- the abstraction relies on traced/freehand contours and irregular blobs rather than recognizable standard geometric primitives, combinations, and controlled deformations;
- any identity-bearing part of the main subject is clipped, omitted, disconnected, or lost between the middle and bottom panels;
- an extremity or ground contact touches the panel edge, is shaved off, merges into foreground geometry, or cannot be traced back to its limb/body;
- a head, face, foot, tail, garment, or other important local whole fragments into unrelated pieces or collapses into an ambiguous undifferentiated mass;
- the middle uses filled masses instead of black line geometry;
- the bottom retains outlines or introduces arbitrary colors with no source or compositional rationale;
- an organic subject is dominated by gratuitous acute triangles, needle-like wedges, sawtooth edges, or abrupt corner transitions;
- uniform smoothing erases characteristic contour breaks, articulation, stance, expression, or material seams;
- the geometry retains avoidable surface detail, ornament, repeated micro-structure, or low-value descriptive divisions; or it is simplified so far that characteristic structure, modular rhythm, material logic, expression, or source-specific pattern is lost; or it lacks an intentional shape family, active negative space, visual focus, or balanced weight;
- background detail, contrast, line density, or color segmentation competes with the main subject, or the background is simplified so far that necessary support and spatial relationships disappear;
- decorative graphics, explanatory text, or generic poster effects compete with the study;
- the abstraction is either a literal trace or so arbitrary that the source structure disappears.

Apply the review gates in this order. Stop at the first failure and rebuild the prompt from the construction plan rather than merely adding another negative instruction:

1. **Subject-and-view gate:** the primary subject is correctly identified, visually dominant, and reconstructed in an orthographic view appropriate to the input. A clear side, rear, or top input must remain the corresponding side, rear, or top orthographic construction. Reject arbitrary rotation to the front, unresolved photographic perspective, or a composition in which the background competes with the subject.
2. **Primitive gate:** at thumbnail and normal size, the primary masses can be named as circles, ellipses, triangles, squares, rectangles, controlled polygons, lines, arcs, points, or clear combinations and deformations of them. If recognition comes mainly from a traced outer contour, fur edge, or detailed silhouette, reject it.
3. **Construction-and-rule gate:** primary local wholes and identity-bearing accessories read as coherent geometry assembled through visible alignment, overlap, tangency, repetition, or intersection. When the subject contains bilateral, central, rotational, radial, repeated, alternating, gridded, or modular structure, the construction must visibly preserve that rule. Decorative geometry placed inside a traced silhouette does not pass.
4. **Transformation-coherence gate:** middle and bottom must match contour-for-contour and region-for-region in exact registration. Reject any added, removed, shifted, rotated, merged, split, rescaled, or redrawn shape in the bottom panel; only line removal and color assignment may differ.
5. **Integrity gate:** all protected parts and ground contacts remain complete and safely inset.
6. **Material gate:** middle is outline-only and bottom is outline-free.

Do not accept a result merely because the top photograph is preserved, the subject is recognizable, or the middle obeys the no-fill rule. Those are necessary but not sufficient.

Also perform a complexity test at thumbnail size and normal viewing size:

- Too simple: identity relies on one generic silhouette; characteristic overlaps, structural subdivisions, modular rhythm, material logic, source-specific pattern, expression, or focal tension are missing; the abstraction could fit many unrelated sources.
- Too complex: minor texture, ornament, low-value repeated construction detail, foliage, fur, individual tiles, paving, or surface marks become geometry; contours multiply without hierarchy; negative space collapses; the image reads as tracing rather than reconstruction. Preserve informative subject structure while removing only low-value detail.
- Balanced: primary masses read instantly, secondary geometry rewards closer viewing, and tertiary accents remain sparse.

Run three additional checks:

- Integrity check: compare the subject integrity envelope across all panels and inspect all four panel edges. The silhouette, supports, appendages, focal features, identity-bearing accessories, and ground contacts must remain complete and mappable, with a visible safety margin.
- Local-whole check: isolate the head/face, each foot/support, tail, and any key garment or prop. Each must read first as one coherent unit, then reveal only useful internal divisions.
- Softness check: for organic subjects, curves and rounded transitions should carry most of the mass, but source-specific structural breaks must remain. Every sharp point needs a reason, and every long smooth contour must still preserve articulation.
- Composition check: squint or view at thumbnail size. The eye should move through one dominant focus, a supporting rhythm, and a counterweight; no region should feel randomly busy or inert.
- Subject-background check: at thumbnail size, the subject must read first through silhouette and contrast. At normal size, background geometry should explain only necessary support, direction, scale, framing, and counterweight; remove any background line or color plane that competes without serving one of those roles.
- View-selection check: confirm that the middle and bottom share one source-appropriate orthographic construction—front, side, rear, top, bottom, or primary structural plane—without accidental camera perspective, while the input's dominant orientation and identity-bearing relationships remain legible.
- Geometric-rule check: identify any genuine bilateral, central, rotational, radial, repeating, alternating, gridded, or modular organization in the subject and verify that the abstraction uses it consistently without inventing false regularity.
- Primitive check: identify the underlying circles, ellipses, triangles, squares, rectangles, polygons, lines, arcs, and points. If most shapes cannot be understood as a standard form, combination, or controlled deformation, reconstruct rather than trace.

Return the generated image and briefly identify the organizing geometry and palette logic. Do not claim an editable vector deliverable unless one was actually created.
