# Aesthetic Criteria

Read this reference before writing the image-generation prompt and again when evaluating the result.

## Structural fidelity

Preserve the source's visual grammar: its weight distribution, dominant axes, relative scale, overlap order, focal point, and characteristic negative spaces. Exact contours are less important than these relationships. A good abstraction remains recognizable at thumbnail size without depending on descriptive detail.

Define a subject integrity envelope before abstracting: outer silhouette, contact/support points, appendages, focal features, and identity-bearing accessories or props. Keep these elements complete and legibly connected in both transformed panels. Do not confuse simplification with amputation. Preserve source occlusion, but do not invent cropping or overlap that makes an essential part disappear.

Add a safety margin around that envelope and audit the top, bottom, left, and right edges separately. Extremities and ground contacts need special protection. A foot or paw is complete only when its outer mass, connection to the limb, local negative space, and supporting ground relationship are all legible. A visible toe is not enough if the heel, sole, or attachment has been clipped or merged away.

## Local-whole construction

Treat important regions as nested compositions rather than collections of parts. Build the whole subject from coherent local units such as head/face, torso, individual feet or supports, tail, garment, and key prop.

For each unit, preserve:

- an enclosing silhouette;
- an internal direction or axis;
- its attachment and overlap relationship;
- one or two identity-bearing landmarks;
- a shared formal logic among its subdivisions, such as related radii, aligned centers, repeated proportions, or tangent continuations.

The head should read as a complete head before eyes, muzzle, ears, or hair modules are noticed. A foot should read as a stable support before toe or cuff divisions are noticed. Simplify within the unit, not at the expense of the unit.

## Economy and hierarchy

Every line or color plane should define identity, support, structure, rhythm, material logic, tension, or emphasis. Merge weak neighboring shapes and remove redundant or merely descriptive divisions. Prefer clear primary forms, a restrained supporting layer of secondary geometry, a few source-specific accents, and generous quiet areas. When two simplification levels both preserve basic recognition and action, prefer the one that better retains characteristic structure, modular rhythm, expression, or pattern without becoming illustrative tracing.

## Subject-background hierarchy

The primary subject or subject group must carry the clearest silhouette, most complete local structures, strongest useful contrast, and highest concentration of informative secondary geometry. Background elements exist to establish support, scale, framing, direction, atmosphere, or counterweight; they should not receive equal descriptive attention.

Simplify backgrounds by category rather than by isolated object. Merge repeated foliage, flowers, stones, architecture, debris, ground marks, and surface texture into one to three larger masses or rhythms whenever possible. Retain only dominant axes, contact planes, framing shapes, and the minimum overlaps needed to locate the subject. Remove internal divisions that merely describe texture or inventory objects. Use wide quiet intervals, restrained contrast, and sparse accents behind the subject.

Preserve context when it is functional: a feeder used by an animal, a branch supporting a bird, the ground beneath feet, or a prop defining an action belongs to the subject system even if it sits spatially in the environment. Simplify peripheral context more aggressively. Do not isolate the subject in an arbitrary empty field when the original setting supplies necessary balance or meaning.

Use the squint test: the subject should register first, the main environmental counterweight second, and background texture should largely disappear. If background lines or color planes command equal attention, merge, remove, lighten, or reduce them.

Treat the identified subject element or group as the visual center of attention. This means perceptual priority, not compulsory placement at the mathematical center. Keep subject geometry crisp, specific, and higher contrast. Simplify or visually soften the background with fewer modules, quieter strokes, broader low-contrast fields, reduced saturation, and, where compatible with flat color, restrained softness or blur.

## Complexity calibration

Judge the abstraction by information value, not by the raw number of shapes.

- Primary forms must state silhouette, weight distribution, and dominant direction.
- Secondary forms must explain important overlaps, joints, internal divisions, or rhythm.
- Tertiary marks may locate a focal point or controlled irregularity; they must not become texture replacement.

Run three tests:

1. Thumbnail test: the main subject and directional structure should remain legible.
2. Substitution test: if the geometry could describe many unrelated sources, restore one or two source-specific relationships.
3. Removal test: remove any line or plane whose absence does not weaken identity, balance, rhythm, or spatial logic.

Prefer roughly 4-7 primary masses, a restrained supporting set of secondary divisions, and only a few tertiary accents. These are calibration ranges, not quotas. A complex architectural or mechanically articulated subject may need more secondary modules; a single simple object may need fewer.

## Geometric authorship

Construct a small family of related primitives and repeat or vary it deliberately. Use alignment, tangency, continuation, interruption, proportion, and counterform to create coherence. Small controlled deviations may retain the source's character; random irregularity does not.

Standard primitives must remain perceptible as the grammar of the image. Build with triangles, circles, ellipses, squares, rectangles, regular or controlled polygons, lines, arcs, and points through repetition, mirroring, rotation, scaling, overlap, nesting, intersection, subtraction, cropping, and restrained deformation. Deformation may stretch, taper, bend, round, truncate, or chamfer a primitive, but should preserve its geometric ancestry. Avoid freehand silhouettes that cannot be explained by the chosen shape family.

Before constructing, detect whether the subject has an inherent geometric rule: bilateral symmetry, central symmetry, rotational symmetry, radial organization, translational repetition, alternating rhythm, a grid, modular proportion, or regular spacing. Use that rule to determine axes, centers, intervals, mirrored parts, repeated modules, and controlled deviations. Preserve meaningful asymmetry and damage; do not force a perfect rule onto an irregular subject.

Use the **blank-silhouette test**: mentally remove all interior divisions from the transformed subject. If what remains is essentially a detailed copy of the photographic outer contour, the work is contour translation rather than geometric reconstruction. Rebuild the enclosing masses from a few primitives, then restore only the source-specific contour events needed for identity or articulation.

Use the **primitive-removal test**: mentally remove the few obvious circles, ellipses, rectangles, or triangles. If the work would still read almost entirely through a traced furry or irregular contour, the primitives are decorative rather than structural. Standard forms must carry the subject's mass, attachment, and proportion.

Use circles and ellipses for centers, rotations, softness, and recurring joints; squares and rectangles for stability, enclosure, architecture, and grids; triangles and tapered polygons for direction, tension, ears, limbs, shadows, and perspective; lines for axes, rhythm, force, and registration; points for rare focal punctuation. Do not use every primitive merely to demonstrate variety.

For organic subjects, let rounded geometry dominate: ellipses, broad arcs, rounded rectangles, softened polygons, tangent joins, and continuous curve transitions. Acute triangles and needle-like wedges should be rare, localized, and justified by the source. Broaden pointed tips or connect them with arcs when doing so preserves identity. Retain a few straight axes for structure so the system feels supple rather than shapeless.

Avoid indiscriminate smoothing. Preserve a limited set of informative contour breaks: joints, plane changes, facial angles, attachment points, garment seams, tail bends, or contact transitions. Use curves for volume, short straights and moderate corners for articulation, and rounded joins for continuity. This soft-hard-soft cadence retains detail without reverting to tracing. If two adjacent parts merge visually after smoothing, restore one structural breakpoint or value separation.

## Source-appropriate orthographic normalization

The middle and bottom panels should analyze the source through the orthographic view best supported by the photograph rather than defaulting to a front view. Remove lens distortion, foreshortening, oblique depth, and accidental camera overlap while preserving the source's dominant structural orientation.

Choose among front, left, right, rear, top, bottom, facade, or primary structural-plane views. A predominantly side-facing animal or object should normally remain a left or right elevation; a rear-facing or overhead subject should remain a rear or top view. For planar or radial subjects—flowers, butterflies, leaves, ferns, fans—present the photographed primary plane square to the viewer. When multiple subjects interact, choose the view that best preserves their photographed roles, action, relative scale, and functional relationship.

View normalization is analysis, not arbitrary invention. Preserve characteristic proportions, parts, meaningful asymmetry, attachment, action, functional props, and directional logic. The middle and bottom must share the same selected view and recognizable formal system, but may differ through purposeful recomposition after perspective correction.

Do not rotate a clear side, rear, or top view to the front merely to reveal more features. When the input lies between standard views, select the nearest informative orthographic view and use only a minimal local reveal for an identity-bearing feature.

## Compositional artistry

Judge the work as a composition, not merely a recognizable conversion. Establish one dominant visual focus, one supporting rhythm, and one counterweight. Organize a related family of shapes through repetition with variation, scale contrast, curved-versus-straight tension, alignment, tangency, continuation, and interruption. Let negative space have deliberate shape and unequal intervals; avoid both accidental holes and evenly spaced decoration.

The strongest area may carry denser intersections or higher contrast, while quieter zones provide rest. Preserve useful asymmetry from the source. Do not solve every subject with a centered emblem, uniform tessellation, generic polygon mesh, or arbitrary Bauhaus-like decoration.

## Constructive simplification

Treat the photograph as visual evidence for the middle panel, not as a contour map. After extracting the subject's main masses, axes, modules, symmetry, rhythm, and negative spaces, simplify deliberately:

- simplify weak descriptive parts into stronger masses while preserving a few identity-bearing subdivisions;
- retain a controlled amount of repetition, mirroring, rotation, interlock, or overlap when it carries a genuine source relationship or characteristic modular rhythm;
- exaggerate scale contrast, diagonal force, interval, or asymmetry when it clarifies the subject's energy;
- select, omit, or consolidate secondary elements to create a dominant focus, supporting rhythm, and counterweight without erasing source-specific structure;
- shape negative space as an active plane rather than leftover background.

Simplification must be motivated by the source's visual logic. Preserve subject identity, canonical orientation, essential action, functional relationships, characteristic landmarks, and a restrained amount of informative articulation, modular repetition, or pattern. Do not preserve photographic contour, surface texture, low-value ornament, exact spacing, or complete one-for-one subdivision. A successful result feels authored and inevitable: more distilled than conventional illustration, more structurally specific than a generic icon.

Choose one primary compositional strategy per image—such as asymmetric balance, diagonal tension, radial expansion, modular repetition, figure-ground interlock, or nested enclosure—and at most one supporting strategy. Avoid combining every constructivist device at once.

## Middle-panel line system

Black lines describe boundaries, implied planes, axes, or movement. Favor clean intersections and intentional endings. Concentrate structural clarity and useful secondary divisions on the subject. Reduce the background to fewer large boundaries, framing lines, support planes, and directional axes; when using tiered stroke weights, keep background lines quieter than subject contours. Avoid the look of automated edge detection, technical CAD diagrams, coloring-book outlines, loose sketching, or dense contour maps.

## Bottom-panel color system

Assign color to the exact closed regions established by the middle panel. Dominant colors establish field and weight; accent colors mark focal tension; neutrals create rest. Do not alter, redraw, subdivide, merge, shift, rotate, or add region boundaries during coloring. Give the subject clearer value separation and the most specific source-derived accents. Keep the background in the same simplified one to three broad regions established in the middle panel, using fewer value steps and restrained contrast. Preserve useful value contrast between adjacent shapes after removing outlines. Do not use a rainbow palette when the source is restrained, or mute a high-chroma source into generic tasteful pastels.

Treat the source palette as a family of color anchors, not a paint-by-number obligation. Build an intentional dominant-support-accent system, usually with one dominant family, one supporting family, one chromatic or neutral counterweight, and one restrained focal accent. Fine adjustments may include:

- shifting neighboring hues toward a shared temperature;
- separating adjacent values so contourless shapes remain legible;
- reducing background saturation and concentrating chroma at the focal subject;
- replacing dead gray with a related chromatic neutral;
- slightly enlarging or reducing a color area's proportion to balance visual weight;
- using simultaneous contrast, complementary tension, analogous harmony, or warm-cool balance when supported by the source.

Use color-composition principles associated with Albers-like interaction studies, modernist poster design, cut-paper clarity, and refined editorial illustration as decision lenses, not as looks to imitate. Do not copy a specific artwork, signature palette, or named living artist's style. The result should feel source-related yet more harmonious, intentional, and visually resonant than literal sampling.

## Transformation coherence

The middle and bottom panels are the same simplified drawing in exact registration. Every outer contour, internal boundary, overlap edge, module, and negative-space boundary must map one-to-one. The bottom differs only by removing black lines and filling those same regions with flat color and limited within-region tonal variation. No secondary module may be recomposed, added, removed, merged, split, shifted, rotated, or rescaled.

Check every protected identity-bearing subject component. When line boundaries disappear in the color panel, retain its role through value contrast, color adjacency, overlap, a breathing gap, or a deliberate merged mass. No limb, support, appendage, focal feature, or characteristic accessory may vanish accidentally because neighboring color fields merge.

Map protected local wholes by identity and function as well as their locked middle-panel subdivision. In particular, verify every head/face unit and each foot/support independently. Their enclosing masses, attachments, essential landmarks, informative internal regions, and supporting negative spaces must survive the line-to-color transition unchanged.

## Art-historical lenses

Choose one or two relevant lenses according to the source rather than combining all of them:

- Piet Mondrian: structural reduction, asymmetric balance, and disciplined relations between verticals and horizontals.
- Kazimir Malevich and El Lissitzky: dynamic geometric tension, rotation, scale contrast, and active negative space.
- Wassily Kandinsky and Paul Klee: visual rhythm, directional energy, point-line-plane relationships, and controlled play.
- Josef Albers: color interaction, value adjacency, and perceptual hierarchy.
- Henri Matisse's cut-paper works: economical silhouette, decisive contour, and color-field clarity.
- Charley Harper: reduction of natural subjects into crisp, recognizable geometric relationships without losing character.
- Saul Bass: strong silhouette, asymmetry, visual metaphor, and bold figure-ground organization.
- Bruno Munari: modular play, functional simplicity, and intelligent transformation between forms.
- Ikko Tanaka and Shigeo Fukuda: distilled forms, spatial wit, controlled contrast, and poster-scale clarity.

These names identify reusable formal principles, not styles to copy. Do not reproduce a specific artwork, signature motif, or exact artist look. When a referenced creator is living or their status is uncertain, describe only the general formal principle and avoid naming them in the generation prompt.

## Selection by source

- Architecture, windows, streets, or grids: Mondrian, Lissitzky, Albers.
- Organic subjects, animals, plants, or expressive motion: Klee, Kandinsky, Harper, Matisse cut-paper.
- Strong silhouette, editorial clarity, or poster-like contrast: Bass, Tanaka, Fukuda.
- Playful objects, transformations, or modular systems: Munari.

Use the chosen lens to improve one or two decisions—such as balance, rhythm, silhouette, or color—not to impose an unrelated historical aesthetic on the source.

## Common failure modes

- A photo collage with decorative line art rather than a visible abstraction process.
- A literal vector trace that preserves too much incidental detail.
- A transformed panel that retains photographic perspective or arbitrary three-quarter distortion instead of a source-appropriate orthographic construction.
- A generic front-view specimen imposed on a clear side, rear, or top-view source.
- A background whose detail, contrast, saturation, or sharpness competes with the identified subject.
- Failure to use a genuine symmetry, radial, repetition, grid, or modular rule that visibly organizes the subject.
- Freehand or blob-like contours whose underlying standard geometric primitives and construction operations are not perceptible.
- Generic Bauhaus motifs unrelated to the supplied image.
- A random mixture of famous-artist signatures with no structural relationship to the source.
- Excessive symmetry that erases the source's tension.
- Thin, fragile linework that loses hierarchy.
- Cropped, missing, disconnected, or merged-away parts of the main subject in either transformed panel.
- Feet, paws, hands, wheels, bases, or other contact elements that touch the panel edge, lack a limb attachment, lose their supporting ground relation, or merge into foreground pattern.
- Heads, feet, tails, garments, or props broken into unrelated decorative fragments rather than coherent local wholes.
- Excessive acute triangles, needle points, sawtooth silhouettes, or brittle polygon meshes on an organic subject.
- Over-smoothed curves that erase joints, facial structure, material seams, expression, stance, or other identity-bearing contour events.
- A recognizable but compositionally inert conversion with no dominant focus, supporting rhythm, counterweight, or shaped negative space.
- An over-reduced subject that reads as a generic icon because characteristic structural bays, joints, facial divisions, wing bands, petal groupings, material seams, or modular rhythms were removed.
- Background foliage, debris, architecture, repeated objects, or surface texture described with equal or greater density and contrast than the subject.
- A background simplified into an unrelated empty field that removes the subject's support, action, scale, or essential spatial context.
- Dark color boundaries that function as hidden outlines.
- Added typography, titles, legends, arrows, or process labels unless explicitly requested.
